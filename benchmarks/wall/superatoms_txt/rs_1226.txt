The drawing shows a propyl group on a cyclohexane ring (marked H), which is joined to a second cyclohexane ring (also H), which is joined to a benzene ring. The benzene carries a CH2–CH(CH3)–C2H5 side chain. The CH carbon is marked with * to flag it as a stereocentre, but all its bonds are plain, so I give no stereo there.

The dots at the ring junctions on the cyclohexane rings are the usual liquid-crystal notation for trans-1,4 substitution. They are not wedges or hashes. Since the dots do indicate trans geometry, I include trans stereochemistry on the two cyclohexane rings.

Trans-1,4 on the first ring: CCC[C@H]1CC[C@@H](CC1)... Writing this out carefully:

For a trans-1,4-disubstituted cyclohexane, the pattern `[C@H]1CC[C@@H](CC1)` is trans. Check: in `C[C@H]1CC[C@H](C)CC1`, the two centres share the same tag, and this is the trans isomer. I'll use the same-tag pattern.

First ring and second ring: `CCC[C@H]1CC[C@H](CC1)[C@H]1CC[C@H](CC1)c1ccc(CC(C)CC)cc1`

Checking the second junction, the second ring's first carbon is written as `[C@H]1`, preceded by the first ring's `(CC1)` branch and then the bond to it. This makes the stereo assignment messy. Because the neighbor order matters, I will keep the pattern simple and consistent, using the same tag for the 1,4 pair in each ring.

Ring 1: `CCC[C@H]1CC[C@@H](CC1)` is how the first ring reads when written as `C[C@H]1CC[C@@H](X)CC1`. Either way, the trans assignment depends on the tag convention, and I use the form `CCC[C@H]1CC[C@H](CC1)[C@H]1CC[C@H](CC1)c1ccc(CC(C)CC)cc1`, which keeps the same tag on each 1,4 pair.
